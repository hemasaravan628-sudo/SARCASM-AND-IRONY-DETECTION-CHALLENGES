# SARCASM-AND-IRONY-DETECTION-CHALLENGES
Sentiment analysis assigns a polarity to opinionated text, and most deployed systems do so by counting or weighting the sentiment-bearing words in it. That strategy quietly assumes that the literal words express what the writer means. Sarcasm and irony break the assumption: “Great, another Monday morning meeting” contains a strongly positive word, yet the writer is complaining. This report examines why sarcasm and irony are hard for sentiment analysis, drawing on published examples, datasets and detection methods, and then tests the problem experimentally with a small Python and NumPy project.
The project compares a lexicon baseline (25 positive and 23 negative words) with a contrast-aware logistic-regression classifier trained from scratch on 40 labelled sentences using eight hand-crafted features. On 12 sarcastic test sentences the lexicon labelled none as negative (10 positive, 2 neutral). At a 0.5 threshold the classifier flagged 10 of the 12 as sarcastic and raised no false alarms on 6 sincere sentences, giving 16 of 18 correct. However, 10 of those 12 sentences were part of the training set, the model misfired on an unseen product-review routing demo (two of three sarcastic reviews were auto-accepted as positive), and it did not understand the two Italian ironic examples that carry no sentiment words. The results confirm that contrast heuristics help with “positive word plus unpleasant situation” sarcasm but cannot replace context, world knowledge and larger annotated corpora.
Keywords: sarcasm detection, irony, sentiment analysis, lexicon baseline, contrast feature, logistic regression, human-in-the-loop.
